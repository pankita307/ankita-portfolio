On Adaptability
Food, freight, electronics, SaaS — how does that actually connect?
The Question I Always Get

Global supply chain consulting, warehouse floors, C-suite priorities, shipped production-grade code — food, freight, electronics, SaaS. The question I get is always some version of: how does that all connect?

It connects through adaptability — not as a personality trait, but as a discipline. The thinking has to change. The industry changes, the company changes, the problems change, and so does the way you need to approach them. Most people instead carry their last environment with them like luggage — they come from semiconductors or retail or consulting and spend their energy trying to reshape the new place into the familiar one, nudging the culture, the process, the tools, back toward what they already know how to do.

I don't do that. I show up, I read the room — the org, the data, the floor, the financials — and I build from what's actually there. The supply chain is different everywhere. That's the point. My job is to walk into that difference and deliver anyway. I always have.

Why Breadth Is Undervalued, Not Overvalued

Recruiting has quietly become its own supply chain problem. ATS systems comb keyword by keyword, looking for something close to a carbon copy of the job description before a resume is ever seen by a person. Every open role feels this, but it shows up hardest in supply chain, where the real skill isn't fluency in any one function — it's understanding the inter-dependencies between commodity markets, cost, quality, planning, and execution, all at once. That's not something you gain overnight, and it's not something you get by keeping one person in one seat for twenty years either.

It's also not only about technical and domain knowledge. Different roles ask for different instincts — speed and on-the-feet decision-making in a crisis, patience and an eye for detail somewhere else, a research mindset and first-principles thinking elsewhere still. I've picked up each of these along the way, and I can tell you exactly where, how, and when I gained them — which is a different kind of claim than listing them because they raise an ATS match score.

Supply Chain Is The Business

I've believed this for a while, and experience has only made me more certain: supply chain is the business. Not a function of it — the thing itself. Every company that turns a profit, whether or not it touches physical goods, is running a supply chain. Operations in tech is a supply chain. A software release cycle is a supply chain. Capital allocation is a supply chain.

I know this isn't just a professional position, because it's how I actually live. I plan the inventory in my house. I time purchases around sales and warehouse clearances. I optimize fuel runs. I milk-run my errands without thinking about it. Bringing that to my work doesn't feel like effort — it feels like the most natural extension of how I already think.

The Track Record

I've consistently been in the top rating bracket in every role I've been reviewed in. Top five in my undergraduate class. Multiple offers coming out of my master's — including a couple before I'd even finished — in one of the toughest hiring markets in recent memory, as an immigrant in the US.

The through-line is the point: the work ethic, the range, the way I think about problems — it holds up across industries, across environments, across whatever gets thrown at it. Food, freight, electronics, SaaS. The supply chain is different everywhere. The thinking is the same.

Problem

Sourcing, freight-mode, and inventory targets were set in separate spreadsheets by separate people, each optimizing their own slice — with no single view of total delivered cost once tariff, freight, and holding costs were added back in.

Approach

Built a mixed-integer linear program minimizing total delivered cost as one model, wired directly to live Databricks, NetSuite, Salesforce, and E2Open data, with container-level freight logic layered on classical inventory theory (EOQ, safety stock, MOQ). General Mills · Senior Supply Chain Analyst · ~2-Year Project
The Allergen Changeover: A Multi-Line Traveling Salesman Problem
Problem

General Mills was paying a third-party co-manufacturer to produce roughly 6M bars a year that could have been made on our own lines — except every changeover between allergen categories eats production time, and nobody had ever precisely mapped how much. Some category-to-category cleans took twenty minutes; others took a day, sometimes two. Multiply that uncertainty across four to six plants, each running different machines with different clean-time physics, and the honest answer to "how much spare capacity do we actually have" was: nobody really knew.

The data problem

The first six months weren't optimization — they were data archaeology. I needed a full matrix of every allergen category against every other allergen category, for every line, at every plant: something close to 40 by 40, one clean-time entry at a time. Most of that knowledge lived in people's heads, not in any system — plant managers, line leads, the workers who actually ran the cleans. Getting it out, validated, and signed off by every plant and every shift took the better part of half a year — and that part was harder than the math that came after it.

The human variable

That data collection surfaced a real modeling decision. I met one line worker who was simply better than the matrix said anyone should be — cutting clean times by 40–50% through sheer skill at multitasking and coordinating his team, in a way his coworkers trusted and responded to. It wasn't universal; it didn't show up on other shifts or other plants. I had to decide whether to bake an outlier like that into the model or treat it as noise, and I chose to optimize against the reliable, repeatable clean times rather than the exceptional ones — because a model that assumes everyone performs like your best person breaks the day that person calls in sick, or worse, leaves the company entirely. It's the kind of judgment call a purely automated system doesn't know it's making.

The model

Once the matrix existed, the real shape of the problem showed up: this wasn't one traveling salesman problem, it was four to six of them running simultaneously — one per plant/line — each sequencing its own loop through allergen categories to minimize changeover time. Except each "city" also had a dwell time: how long to run one allergen profile before the next clean, which is really a capacity decision. Run too long and you build inventory you don't have the shelf life or warehouse space for. Run too short and you're cleaning more than you're producing. The model had to solve sequencing and batch-sizing together, across every plant, at once.

The geography constraint

Even a perfect changeover schedule at one plant can create a bad outcome for the network. US demand isn't evenly distributed — it's concentrated on the coasts, especially the East, with a lot of empty geography in the middle. One of our highest-capacity plants sat closer to the West Coast. Producing more there just because a line had spare capacity could mean shipping cross-country to where demand actually was — trading a manufacturing win for a freight loss. Plant-level KPIs (yield, uptime, changeover time) don't see that trade-off. An end-to-end planner has to see it anyway.

Result

The redesigned changeover sequencing freed 20–30 additional days of manufacturing capacity across the network — enough to internalize roughly 6M bars a year previously made by a third-party co-manufacturer, worth about $2M/year.

What it taught me

This is the project I'd talk about for decades to come, regardless of whatever bigger things I go on to achieve. Two years, and I loved almost every hour of the complexity, because it was never just an optimization problem — it was a data-archaeology problem, a change-management problem, a judgment call about when to trust an exceptional person over an average one, a geography problem, and a multi-stakeholder program-management problem, all stacked on top of a genuinely fun operations-research core. I'm immensely grateful I got to run this early in my career — it shaped my foundations in a way nothing else could have, and gave me the intuition and skills I now use daily, regardless of industry or the nature of the problem in front of me. That foundation is exactly the asset I carry into this ever-changing world of autonomous AI and agents.

Independent Study · Self-Initiated, Unpaid
Testing my frameworks in an industry I'd never worked in
Why

Does the way I think about operations problems — cost trade-offs, process maturity, data readiness — transfer to an industry I know nothing about? FIBC manufacturing (industrial woven bags used across chemicals, agriculture, and construction) was a good stress test: physical, global, cost-sensitive, and nowhere near my résumé. No client asked for this, and there was no deadline.

Approach

Built a full discovery toolkit from scratch — a process swimlane, a structured interview guide, a 1–5 process-maturity scoring model, a data inventory, and a prioritized gap log — using a real manufacturer as the case study, from public information and conversations I initiated myself. Translated it into an AI-transformation strategy: quick-win ROI use cases, a build-vs-buy framework, and a phased 12-month roadmap.

Result

A complete, end-to-end AI-transformation strategy built for a manufacturer I had no prior relationship with, using only public information and self-initiated conversations.

What it taught me

The frameworks travel. The savings numbers change by industry, but the discipline — audit the data before you buy the model, find the 90-day win before the 12-month one — held up somewhere with zero overlap with food, freight, or hardware.
